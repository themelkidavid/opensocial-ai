# Innovation Discovery Engine: Worked Example

## Purpose

This example demonstrates how the Innovation Discovery Engine can analyse a social-sector problem and develop new, testable solution hypotheses.

The example is fictional and uses no real beneficiary or organisational data.

---

# Problem

A community organisation observes that young people who are eligible for an important health and social service are not consistently accessing it.

Existing programme reports show that:

- Awareness of the service is relatively high.
- Initial contact with the programme is reasonable.
- However, continued engagement is significantly lower.
- Drop-off varies between locations.
- Traditional awareness campaigns have produced only limited improvement.

The conventional response might be to increase awareness activities.

The Innovation Discovery Engine asks:

> **Is lack of awareness actually the main problem?**

---

# 1. Problem Reframing

Instead of defining the problem as:

> "Young people do not know about the service."

the engine reframes it as:

> **"Young people may know about the service but encounter barriers between awareness, first use and continued engagement."**

This changes the investigation from an awareness problem to a potential **journey and retention problem**.

---

# 2. Historical Data Analysis

Suppose an organisation provides five years of anonymised programme data.

The engine examines:

- First-contact rates
- Repeat visits
- Drop-off points
- Geographic differences
- Age groups
- Referral sources
- Waiting times
- Service hours
- Follow-up patterns
- Feedback from young people

The objective is to identify patterns rather than simply produce averages.

---

# 3. Possible Patterns

The engine identifies several hypothetical patterns requiring validation:

### Pattern A

Initial engagement is relatively high, but repeat engagement falls sharply.

### Pattern B

Drop-off appears higher in locations where travel time is greater.

### Pattern C

Young people referred through peers appear to remain engaged longer than those referred through formal channels.

### Pattern D

Drop-off may be higher when service appointments conflict with education or employment schedules.

### Pattern E

Young people report concerns about confidentiality and being recognised when accessing services.

These observations do not establish causation.

They generate questions for further investigation.

---

# 4. Knowledge Gaps

The analysis identifies several important unknowns:

- Why do people discontinue after first contact?
- Is transport a major barrier?
- Does service timing affect engagement?
- Does fear of disclosure affect behaviour?
- Does the experience differ between service locations?
- Do peer referrals create greater trust?
- Are young people avoiding formal systems because of stigma?
- Which interventions have previously been attempted?
- Why did earlier approaches succeed or fail?

---

# 5. Competing Root-Cause Hypotheses

The engine avoids assuming there is one cause.

Possible explanations include:

### Hypothesis 1: Access

Physical distance or transport makes continued engagement difficult.

### Hypothesis 2: Time

Existing service schedules do not fit young people's daily lives.

### Hypothesis 3: Trust

Young people may not feel sufficiently safe or confident using formal services.

### Hypothesis 4: Confidentiality

Fear of being recognised may discourage continued engagement.

### Hypothesis 5: Experience

The first interaction with the service may not meet expectations.

### Hypothesis 6: Social Influence

Peer behaviour and social networks may strongly influence continued engagement.

These hypotheses should be tested against evidence.

---

# 6. Cross-Sector Exploration

The engine then looks beyond the immediate programme sector.

Potentially relevant ideas could come from:

- Mobile banking
- E-commerce
- Education technology
- Telemedicine
- Youth engagement platforms
- Behavioural science
- Community development
- Customer-retention systems

The purpose is not to copy another sector.

The purpose is to identify principles that could potentially transfer.

---

# 7. Solution Hypotheses

The engine generates several possible approaches.

## Solution A: Flexible Service Windows

Offer selected services during evenings or weekends.

### Why it might work

It may reduce conflicts with education and employment.

### Risk

Additional staffing and operational costs.

### Test

Pilot flexible hours at a small number of locations.

---

## Solution B: Peer Navigation

Train trusted young community members to act as peer navigators.

### Why it might work

Peer relationships may reduce uncertainty and increase trust.

### Risk

Peer navigators could unintentionally expose confidential information.

### Test

Develop strict confidentiality protocols and test the approach with a small group.

---

## Solution C: Low-Visibility Access

Explore service models that allow people to access support without unnecessary public visibility.

### Why it might work

It could reduce perceived stigma or fear of disclosure.

### Risk

A poorly designed system could create new privacy problems.

### Test

Co-design the approach with young people before implementation.

---

## Solution D: Digital Pre-Engagement

Allow people to obtain basic information, ask questions and understand the service process before attending in person.

### Why it might work

Uncertainty may be a barrier to first or repeat engagement.

### Risk

Digital access may exclude people without reliable connectivity.

### Test

Offer digital and non-digital pathways simultaneously.

---

## Solution E: Community Micro-Hubs

Instead of requiring every interaction to occur at a central facility, explore trusted community locations for selected non-sensitive activities.

### Why it might work

It may reduce travel barriers.

### Risk

Privacy and confidentiality must be carefully protected.

### Test

Pilot only activities that can safely occur outside the main service environment.

---

# 8. Challenge the Solutions

The engine should now challenge its own ideas.

For example:

### Flexible hours

What if time is not the main barrier?

### Peer navigation

What if young people do not trust peers with sensitive concerns?

### Digital access

What if digital tools increase rather than reduce exclusion?

### Community micro-hubs

Could greater proximity unintentionally reduce privacy?

### Low-visibility services

Could the design make the service harder for people to understand?

This stage is critical.

The objective is not to make the ideas look good.

The objective is to discover whether they survive serious questioning.

---

# 9. Prioritisation

Potential approaches can be compared using a structured framework.

| Solution | Potential Impact | Feasibility | Evidence Needed | Risk | Novelty |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flexible service windows | High | Medium | Medium | Low | Medium |
| Peer navigation | High | Medium | High | Medium | High |
| Low-visibility access | High | Medium | High | High | High |
| Digital pre-engagement | Medium | High | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Community micro-hubs | High | Low | High | High | High |

These ratings are illustrative.

They should not be treated as evidence.

---

# 10. Human and Community Validation

Before implementation, the organisation should ask:

- Do young people recognise these barriers?
- Which barriers matter most?
- Which proposed solutions would actually be used?
- Which solutions could create unintended harm?
- What would make a person trust the service?
- What would make someone stop using it?
- What would confidentiality mean from the user's perspective?
- Which ideas are culturally and socially appropriate?

Community participation is essential.

---

# 11. Small Pilot

Rather than implementing every idea simultaneously, the organisation could select one or two promising hypotheses.

For example:

### Pilot

Test flexible service hours combined with peer navigation in a limited number of locations.

### Duration

A defined pilot period.

### Measures

Measure:

- First-time engagement
- Repeat engagement
- Drop-off
- User satisfaction
- Waiting time
- Cost
- Safety and privacy incidents
- Community feedback

The pilot should establish clear criteria for continuing, modifying or stopping the intervention.

---
